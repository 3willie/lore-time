# Grandpa's Initials

It was {{dad}} who earned the next place.

The boys didn't see it happen. They only heard about it at dinner. On the way home from work, {{dad}} had stopped to help an old man change a flat tire in the rain. He'd gotten soaked. He'd missed his bus. He didn't make a big deal about it.

"Anybody would have," he said, shrugging.

That night, the map drew something new. Not at the edge, where it usually drew. Right in the middle, close to their house. A tall, crooked tree with a little wooden house in its branches.

*The Treehouse of Lost Summers.*

"Dad has to come," said {{brother}} immediately. "It's *his* one."

{{dad}} laughed when they told him. Then he stopped laughing, and looked at the map for a long time.

"Grandpa used to say the map wasn't just for kids," he said quietly. "I never believed him."

That night, three hands touched the ink tree together.

The treehouse was real. Its ladder was made of old rope. Its floor was soft with moss. Its windows looked out over a meadow where it was always the last warm evening of summer, with fireflies blinking in the long grass.

And carved into the wall, in the wobbly letters of a child, were names. Dozens of them. Some old and faded, some newer.

{{dad}} ran his fingers along them. Then he stopped.

There, near the bottom, were two sets of initials. One was Grandpa's. And right beside it, much smaller, much wobblier, were {{dad}}'s own.

"I was here," he whispered. "I came here. With him. I was younger than you." He sat down slowly on the mossy floor. "I'd forgotten. How did I forget?"

{{hero}} sat down next to him. "Maybe grown-ups just get busy," he said.

"Maybe," said {{dad}}. His eyes were wet, but he was smiling. "He was a good dad. He used to say: *the map draws what you earn, but you have to keep earning it.* I think I stopped. For a while."

"You helped that man with his tire," said {{brother}}. "So you started again."

{{dad}} pulled both boys into a hug. Then he took a little pocketknife out of his pocket.

Very carefully, underneath his own initials, he carved two new sets. {{hero}}'s. And {{brother}}'s.

When they woke up in the morning, {{dad}} was already in the kitchen, humming, making pancakes for no reason at all.
